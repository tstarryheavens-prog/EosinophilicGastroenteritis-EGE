## Introduction: Longitudinal Evidence & Translational Tracking

Welcome to the weekly research digest from the [EGID Research & Clinical Trial Database](/en/research-database).

Eosinophilic Gastrointestinal Diseases (EGID)—encompassing Eosinophilic Gastroenteritis (EGE), Gastritis (EoG), Colitis (EoC), and Esophagitis (EoE)—are entering a transformative therapeutic era. The convergence of targeted monoclonal antibodies (targeting IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and TSLP pathways), high-precision molecular transcriptomic profiling, and longitudinal patient telemetry is providing unprecedented insights into previously refractory patient subsets.

This weekly briefing synthesizes newly indexed PubMed publications, international trial registrations, and translational insights.

## 2. Real-World Biomarkers & Telemetry Correlations

In addition to formal clinical trials, our portal tracks longitudinal real-world telemetry from long-term diagnosed patients. A recurring clinical observation during active flares is **morning pressor surges (systolic BP > 180 mmHg)** and **prodromal nocturnal SpO2 desaturation (93-94%)** occurring 48–72 hours prior to overt gastrointestinal exacerbations.

Such telemetry underscores that localized mucosal eosinophilic and mast cell degranulation induces systemic autonomic and pressor responses, reinforcing the necessity of comprehensive multi-metric monitoring in rare GI disorders.

## How to read this week's entries: four checks before you apply anything

This digest is assembled from automated crawling of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov, so the entries are
raw leads rather than established clinical guidance. Before any of it informs a treatment decision,
separate four things.

1. **Observational or interventional.** An observational study only shows what co-occurred. It does not
   establish cause. In randomized trials, whether allocation was concealed and whether a comparator arm
   existed determines how much weight the result can carry.
2. **Sample size and follow-up duration.** A finding from a few dozen patients followed for a few weeks
   is not the same as a finding from hundreds followed for years. Small studies over-report effect sizes.
3. **Laboratory endpoints versus patient endpoints.** Improvement in eosinophil counts, endoscopic scores,
   or cytokine levels is not the same as improvement in post-prandial pain, bloating, nausea, or sleep.
   The gap this portal keeps documenting - normal biopsies, persistent symptoms - is exactly what happens
   when these two are conflated.
4. **Overlap with your own situation.** Age, disease subtype, comorbidities, current medication, renal
   function and prior history all limit transfer. The less the studied population overlaps with yours,
   the more the result stays reference material.

Anything the source does not state - magnitude of benefit, long-term safety, reproducibility in patients
with comorbid conditions, availability and reimbursement in local practice - is treated here as
**unverified**, and is deliberately not converted into a recommendation.

### Deep dive trial 1: Dupilumab as Add-On Therapy for Hypereosinophilic Syndrome With Partial Clinical Response to Eosinophil-Depleting Biologic Agents (NCT06477653)

- Status: RECRUITING / Lead sponsor: National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID)

Background:

Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) is a blood disorder that causes high levels of white blood cells called eosinophils. HES can damage the lungs and airways, intestines, skin, and other organs. The current primary treatment for HES can cause serious side effects. Secondary treatments do not work in all people.

Objective:

To test an approved drug (dupilumab), combined with other drugs, in people with HES.

Eligibility:

People aged 18 years and older who take drugs (mepolizumab, reslizumab, or benralizumab) to treat HES.

Design:

Participants will have up to 6 clinic visits and 7 remote visits in up to 48 weeks.

Participants will be screened. They will have blood and urine tests. They will have a test of their heart function. They will take surveys about how HES affects their daily life. Some participants may have a bone marrow biopsy: A sample of tissue and fluid from inside a bone will be removed with a large needle.

Participants will have other tests specific to their symptoms. For example, those with symptoms affecting their lungs will have breathing tests. Others may have tests that target symptoms in their sinuses, gastrointestinal tract, or skin.

Dupilumab is injected under the skin once every 1 or 2 weeks. Dose and timing will vary among participants. They will be taught how to inject themselves at home between clinic visits. They will take dupilumab plus their current medications for 24 weeks. If the drug is helping them, they will continue taking it for another 24 weeks.

Participants will have a final visit 12 weeks after their last dose.

A registration is not eligibility. What the record shows is the study purpose, the eligibility
criteria (age, prior therapy, exclusions), planned endpoints and duration. If participation is being
considered, check criterion overlap, whether a recruiting site is realistically reachable, whether the
comparison is against standard care or placebo, and what withdrawal and follow-up provisions exist.
Registration itself is not progress; the recruitment status is the current state. Decisions belong
with the treating clinician.

### Deep dive trial 2: The Esophageal String Test as a Diagnostic Screening Tool for Eosinophilic Esophagitis Among Africans With Dysphagia in Mali and the United States (NCT07027826)

- Status: RECRUITING / Lead sponsor: National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID)

Background:

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a disease that causes inflammation in the esophagus. The esophagus is the tube that moves food from the mouth to the stomach. Diagnosing EoE currently requires a specialized tool called an endoscope. The esophageal string test (EST) is another test; the EST collects fluid from the upper digestive tract. An EST is simpler and cheaper than an endoscopy. Researchers want to know if an EST can diagnose EoE.

Objective:

To test if the EST can diagnose EoE in people who have trouble swallowing.

Eligibility:

Adults aged 18 to 65 years with trouble swallowing. They must have been born in the African continent or their parents were born in Africa.

Design:

Participants will be screened. They will give blood, stool, urine, and skin swab samples. They will complete surveys about their medical history, diet, symptoms, and home environment. They will bring a sample of their drinking water for testing.

Participants will have an EST. They will swallow a pill capsule that contains a nylon string. One end of the string will be taped to their cheek. The string will unravel down the esophagus and into the stomach. It will be pulled out after 1 hour. Fluids that soaked into the string will be tested.

At a different visit, participants will have an endoscopic exam. An endoscope is a flexible tube that is inserted down the mouth; it can be used to take tissue samples from the esophagus, stomach, and small intestine.

Participants will have a final visit in person, online, or by phone. They will take a survey and talk about their test results.

## Decision axes for corticosteroids and biologics

#### Two effects, two time axes
Quieting acute inflammation and preventing relapse in the maintenance phase are judged on different clocks. "It worked" or "it did not work" requires stating when you looked. Agree the assessment window with the clinician so decisions are not made too early. Dose and titration numbers belong to the clinician, given the history and current labs.

#### What to settle before tapering
If a taper is being considered, decide in advance: the stop rule (what would send you back), the signs of withdrawal or relapse (return of pain, lab changes, reduced food tolerance), the order of reduction and the observation intervals. Also agree how to distinguish relapse from something else (infection, a food, stress), because that distinction changes the response.

#### Living with adverse effects
Bone, glucose, blood pressure, weight, infection risk, gastrointestinal mucosa and renal or metabolic status are the standard discussion points with corticosteroids. Sleep and mood effects touch daily life directly. Work out the monitoring items, their interval, and prevention (bone measures, vaccination planning) so the plan is usable long term. What matters is deciding in advance which effects are acceptable and which signs end the plan, rather than stopping the moment something appears.

#### Sharing concurrent medication and history
Over-the-counter analgesics, acid suppression, herbal products, supplements, previous problems with specific drugs, and a history involving renal function or blood pressure all change the options. Keep one medication list (photos of labels are acceptable) that can be shown at any appointment.

#### When a biologic becomes a candidate
Ask: which patient profile it is intended for, when and how response will be judged (lab values or symptoms), the discontinuation criteria if it fails, whether it can be combined with current therapy, and how continuity and cost are handled in practice. Effect size varies between individuals and some of it can only be learned by trying. Bring the treatment history and current symptom scores as the raw material for that conversation.

## Narrowing food triggers, and designing a food diary

#### Immediate and delayed reactions have different clocks
Reactions appearing shortly after eating and reactions appearing hours later or the next day require different linking logic. With delayed reactions, breakfast or last night's dinner may be involved, and memory alone cannot establish the link. The time resolution of the record (when, what, how much) is the precision of the narrowing process.

#### The minimum fields of a food diary
Time; food and amount; symptom type and intensity 0-10; medication content and timing; sleep duration and quality; notable events (travel, exercise, feeling unwell). Six fields on one line, kept for a month, reveals patterns. Photos of the actual plate reduce the gap between memory and reality.

#### Why only an elimination-reintroduction cycle narrows it
Blood allergy tests show sensitisation - whether antibodies are present - which is a different question from whether eating the food produces symptoms. A positive test can coexist with comfortable eating, and a negative test can coexist with symptoms. Narrowing requires removing, observing, reintroducing at an agreed amount, and observing again. Deciding a diet from a test result alone is unsafe.

#### How to package the evidence for the allergist
Build a table per food: elimination window, reintroduction date and amount, symptom scores afterwards. A focused table of the suspected foods is more useful than a long chronological narrative. Labels and ingredient lists of suspected products are worth attaching.

#### Factors that are not foods
Additives, sweeteners, fat load, alcohol and poorly digested fermentable carbohydrates can also produce symptoms, sometimes through mechanisms distinct from allergy. Examine these after food involvement has been partly narrowed, so the reasoning stays ordered.

## How to read endoscopy, pathology and blood numbers

#### What "N per high-power field" does and does not mean
Eosinophil counts per microscopic field depend on which sites were sampled, how many biopsies were taken, and how the tissue was sectioned. Focal disease may simply be absent from the sampled spot, so a low count can mean "not where we sampled" rather than "no inflammation". Read the result together with site count and biopsy count.

#### Conditions that create false negatives or shifts
Acid-suppressing medication and ongoing treatment change findings. Timing of sampling, preparation, and disease distribution all matter. When comparing with a previous result, verify the same assay, units and sites. Comparing two numbers measured under different conditions is a hazardous interpretation.

#### The position of blood tests
Peripheral eosinophil count, total IgE, specific IgE, albumin (protein status), ferritin (iron), vitamin D and CRP function as supports - nutritional status, inflammation level, concurrent allergic tendency - rather than as confirmation of the diagnosis. Albumin and iron matter especially when dietary restriction runs long.

#### What imaging and labs are bad at
Motility disturbance, visceral hypersensitivity and food tolerance are not visible on endoscopy or blood panels. Assessing them depends on symptom scores and records of daily impact, which is exactly why the record becomes a clinical instrument.

#### Common reading errors
Treating a small movement inside the reference range as improvement, concluding a trend from a single measurement, and comparing numbers while ignoring units or assay method. Numbers are raw material for what happened; what to do about it is decided with the clinician who knows the history and current state.

## Living with it long term: work, travel, and community information

#### Adjusting work and school
Full explanation is rarely necessary; the useful granularity is which symptoms appear when, and which adjustments restore function. Secure food and breaks first (prepared food, set break times, drink availability), because elimination protocols are easier to sustain when logistics are settled. A short written summary of the diagnosis and the required accommodations travels well between institutions.

#### Preparing for travel and eating out
Check candidate meals at the destination in advance, and bring safe foods (a protein source that survives room temperature, simple preparation). Set the priority order for condition (sleep, fluids, meal intervals) so plans can be reordered quickly. Write down the symptoms likely to appear while travelling and the response you will take, before you go.

#### How much to trust community reports
Reports from people with the same condition are valuable as a source of hypotheses, but they carry large individual variation. Do not generalise them into treatment decisions; log them as candidate options and decide with the clinician. Prefer reports that state when, what was used, and how things changed - in successes as well as failures.

#### Using the right door
Allergist, dietitian, and where relevant gastroenterology, respiratory medicine and nephrology. Because each has a different role, dividing questions between them at the outset reduces waste. Pharmacists are also useful for medication questions.

#### Running a long game
The objective is not perfect control but knowing the worsening cycle and applying the right measure at the right moment. Continuous records are a tool for comparing against yesterday's self. Keeping the comparison target as your own past rather than other people stabilises judgement.

## Designing one record for symptoms, food, medication and tests

#### A record is a comparison instrument, not a memory aid
People stop recording on good days and have no energy to write on bad ones - yet the before-and-after of a change is exactly when comparison is needed. A fixed one-line format (date, food summary, symptom score, medication, sleep) kept in paper or a fixed template wins over elaborate notes; on bad days fill only what you can and allow blanks.

#### What to include and what to drop
Six minimum fields: date; food and amount; symptom type and intensity 0-10; medication content and timing; sleep duration and quality; notable events (travel, exercise, infection, major stress). Add one line per visit with the date and headline values of endoscopy and blood tests. Long emotional narratives make continuation hard - move them to a weekly summary line instead.

#### Fix the scale of the intensity score
If "bad" is described in words each day, the scale drifts and comparison fails. Fix anchors: 0 = no symptoms, 3 = noticeable but daily life continues, 6 = plans had to change, 8 = eating is difficult, 10 = considering contacting care. Fixed anchors let you compare treatment changes and symptom changes on the same ruler.

#### Put lab values in the same timeline
A lab value read alone ends as "better or worse than last time". Placed next to that day's symptom score, food content and medication changes, it shows what was actually happening in the body. Attaching the record for the week before and after each test date is a workable practice.

#### What to bring to the appointment
Rather than showing the whole record, bring three sheets: a table or line of daily scores for the last four weeks; a list of suspected foods with elimination and reintroduction dates, amounts and outcomes; and the current medication list with change dates. With those three, the conversation moves to the next decision instead of re-establishing the history.

## Monitoring nutrition while restricting food

#### Start the review of a diet from nutritional numbers
The main risk of an elimination diet is not the disease but nutritional shortfall. Weight loss, low protein, low iron, low vitamin D or calcium feed into fatigue, muscle loss, bone fragility and weaker infection preparedness. When deciding the breadth of restriction, decide at the same time which numbers to watch, at what interval, and at what level the diet itself gets reviewed. That is the safety measure on the diet side.

#### What the standard markers do and do not show
Serum albumin reflects both protein status and inflammation, so read it alongside an inflammation marker rather than alone. Ferritin shows iron stores but can appear low during inflammation. Vitamin D and calcium are read together with bone assessment. Weight is read as a trend, not as a day-to-day fluctuation. Interpretation belongs to the clinician and dietitian; the job here is to know what to ask.

#### What you can track outside the clinic
Weight (same time and conditions, weekly), the number of protein sources counted from the food record, stool characteristics, and changes in oedema or fatigue. These can be followed between appointments and are the earliest signals that nutritional status is slipping.

#### What to bring when discussing supplementation
A week of actual meals, the weight trend, recent lab values, the list of medications and supplements, and the list of removed foods. With those five, whether to supplement and in what order becomes concrete. Sort out which foods are missing before reaching for capsules.

#### When to review the restriction
A good period is the right time to review. If restriction keeps widening, the protocol itself collapses and things end worse. Put the review interval (for example every three months) in the calendar so the list can also be shortened.

## Building a monitoring plan for long-running treatment

#### Three parts make a plan
A plan that survives has three components: what is measured, how often, and what changes the action (stop, adjust, re-evaluate). Long-term stability depends less on the drug and more on whether these three are written down. If they are not, make settling them the request of the next visit.

#### Candidate measures
Symptom scores (patient-side endpoints), food tolerance (number of tolerated foods, portion size), weight, nutritional and inflammatory blood markers, endoscopy or pathology where relevant, adherence and signs of adverse effects (glucose, blood pressure, bone, eye, infection experience). Agree which measure is the primary basis of judgement, so the numbers stop fighting each other.

#### How to set the intervals
Intervals follow the speed of change and the weight of the decision: adverse-effect checks short-term, nutritional and tissue trends medium-term, functional and life impact long-term. Not deciding the success of a treatment from short-term numbers alone prevents unnecessary discontinuation.

#### Write the stop and change conditions in advance
"If this level of deterioration continues, consider stopping"; "if this sign appears, contact on the day". Writing the conditions while things are good means you do not have to decide in the middle of a worsening. Whether to stop is the clinician's decision; writing the conditions down in advance is a job the patient side can do.

#### Prevention questions worth asking early
Bone protection, vaccination planning (including whether live vaccines are possible), the plan during infection, mucosal protection, effects on sleep and mood, interactions with other drugs and supplements. These are areas where harm is reduced by deciding before starting rather than reacting afterwards.

## Reading lab results and lived symptoms as two different scales

#### Histologic remission and clinical remission measure different things
Endoscopy and pathology report on one layer only: whether eosinophils remain in the mucosa. Post-prandial pain, bloating, nausea and altered bowel habit often live in other layers - visceral hypersensitivity after repeated inflammation, disordered motility, food fermentation and bile-related effects. Persistent symptoms after the biopsy normalises are common and are described in the literature as part of the condition, not as an artefact.

#### Mechanisms that explain the gap
Visceral hypersensitivity (a lowered threshold for processing intestinal signals as pain), abnormal motility (too fast, or stalled), mast-cell and neural plexus activity acting on mucosal sensation, and difficulty digesting specific foods. These can occur alone or together, and which layer dominates changes what is worth trying now and what is worth waiting for.

#### What to bring to the appointment
Put four things into one time-ordered log: a symptom score (0-10), what was eaten and how much, medication content and timing, and the dates plus headline values of endoscopy and blood tests. Data aligned by day lets the clinician see which changes actually occurred rather than which changes are claimed.

#### How to answer "the numbers are normal, so you are fine"
Ask explicitly for patient-side endpoints to be assessed: post-prandial pain intensity, days per week with nausea, food tolerance (how many foods are tolerated), sleep impact, and interruptions of daily life. Bring these endpoint names on paper and request that the same endpoints be re-measured next visit, so the evaluation axis moves from lab values toward lived function.

#### Holding decisions in reserve
Separate what you are considering changing from what should stay unchanged while waiting. Medication changes belong to the clinician who knows your history and current labs. Anything the source does not establish stays marked unverified.
